Judgment vs. Discernment

Post
Judgment vs. Discernment
Published
September 4, 2026

The Seattle Freeze

I had a call a few weeks ago with someone I had never met for a new project. As a world-class people pleaser I take pride in my first impression, and I have a pretty impressive record of people generally liking me.

Not this guy. Ice cold. None of my staples worked: warm smile, active listening, subtle compliments, a little self-deprecating humor? Unimpressed.

My conversation with my wife later that evening went something like, “Yeah this guy was clearly not a fan of me, but he was from Seattle, and you know how icy Seattle people are...”

I thought maybe that sounded a little judgmental, but decided to rebrand it “discerning” and move on with my day.

The Rebrand

I do this all the time. Why accept something bad when we can claim that it’s actually good? A tiny beat-up house is advertised as “a cozy home with lots of potential.” The car doesn’t have a bad suspension, the salesman informs you that it just really “grips the road.” When asked about a quirk in the app, the developer confidently states that it’s “a feature, not a bug.”

We rebrand products to make them appealing to a buyer, and we also rebrand our reactions to make them more palatable to ourselves. Guilty. As. Charged.

Discernment vs. Judgment

I aim to be a discerning person. It sounds nice and wise. Judgmental on the other hand, that doesn’t feel so nice. But what’s the actual difference?

When I think of discernment, the picture that comes to mind is a wine sommelier. Their job is less about determining if a wine is good or bad, and more about describing it. I imagine them coming up to my table and informing me that I should be tasting notes of black cherry, and cedar, followed by subtler notes of tobacco, and warm spice. Contrast this with the waiter who asks what you’ll be having and instinctively responds “excellent choice.” One opens an investigation, the other closes the case.

Judgment is convenient. “He’s a narcissist.” It’s a blunt sorting instrument: this or that, good or bad, in or out. There is so much information in this world, judgment allows us to compress it into something digestible. The issue is that it’s lossy compression. And when it comes to people, it’s hard to decompress a label back into a person.

Discernment on the other hand is like a scalpel. It allows you to dissect, understand, and ask better questions. When a co-worker continues to miss deadlines, it’s easy to call them unreliable. It’s harder to be precise with your observations. “He is able to solve extremely complex problems, but struggles to give realistic timelines.” It creates room for a person to be complex. Each observation unlocks a new area of investigation. “What causes that behavior?” “What are they thinking when they do that?” “Are they always like this, or only in this setting?” Each person becomes irreducibly complex, a mystery begging to be explored.

Why It Matters

Understanding opens the door for love. That’s why “known and loved” are often talked about together. Ender’s Game is one of my favorite books and sums it up well:

I think it’s impossible to really understand somebody, what they want, what they believe, and not love them the way they love themselves.

— Orson Scott Card, Ender’s Game

Judgment on the other hand ends the conversation. For me, it ended the conversation with myself as to why my co-worker might not immediately like me. “He’s just icy” seemed easier than self-reflection.

My Personal Test

To be fair to judgment, sometimes you do need to close the case. Maybe you need to make a hiring decision by Friday. But in most cases, you don’t need to pass a verdict on a person.

Question 1: Does something require me reaching a verdict?

If the answer is no (which it often is).

Question 2: Does this observation spark curiosity, or kill it?

As for the icy co-worker. I’m still not convinced that he likes me, but at least now I’m curious as to why.
